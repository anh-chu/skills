# Mechanics and formatting rules

Use this reference when grammar, punctuation, numbers, dates, units, or detailed formatting matter.

## Capitalization

- Use standard US English capitalization unless the project specifies another locale.
- Avoid unnecessary capitals.
- Use sentence case for general titles, headings, captions, table headings, and labels.
- Preserve official capitalization for product names, code, UI labels, and proper nouns.
- Avoid all caps except for established abbreviations, code, and placeholders.
- Avoid camel case in ordinary prose unless reproducing an official or code identifier.

## Abbreviations

- Spell out an unfamiliar abbreviation on first use, followed by the abbreviation in parentheses.
- Do not expand universally familiar abbreviations for the target audience when expansion adds noise.
- Treat common shortened words such as "app" as ordinary words.
- Do not use abbreviations as verbs.
- Choose `a` or `an` based on pronunciation, not spelling.
- Pluralize abbreviations as normal words; do not use an apostrophe to form a plural.

## Articles and pronouns

- Include `a`, `an`, and `the` when standard English requires them, including headings.
- Make pronoun antecedents explicit.
- Prefer "this setting" or "these values" over bare "this" or "these" when ambiguity is possible.
- Use singular "they" when gender is unknown or irrelevant.

## Commas

- Use the serial comma.
- Use a comma after an introductory word or phrase when it improves parsing.
- Use a comma between independent clauses joined by a coordinating conjunction unless the clauses are very short and unambiguous.
- Use a comma before a nonrestrictive `which` clause.

## Colons, semicolons, and dashes

- Use a colon after a complete sentence to introduce a list, explanation, or related material.
- Do not force a colon after a fragment such as "The options are:" if a complete-sentence introduction is easy to write.
- Prefer periods to semicolons when two short sentences are easier to read.
- Use a semicolon for closely related independent clauses or complex list items only when it materially improves clarity.
- Use an em dash for a strong interruption; do not use spaces around it in US-style technical prose.
- Do not use en dashes as a generic substitute for `to` or a hyphen.

## Hyphens

- Hyphenate compound modifiers before a noun when needed to prevent misreading.
- Do not hyphenate an `-ly` adverb with the word it modifies unless an exceptional established spelling requires it.
- Prefixes are usually closed up, but use a hyphen when needed for readability, with proper nouns/numbers, or in established forms such as `self-`.
- Avoid long compound-modifier chains; rewrite instead.

## Parentheses and quotation marks

- Do not hide essential information in parentheses.
- Keep parentheticals short; promote longer thoughts to sentences.
- Do not use optional plurals such as `file(s)`; rewrite.
- Use straight quotation marks in source-like technical content.
- Use double quotation marks for ordinary quotations and titles of short works when they are not links.
- Do not add quotation marks around text already formatted as code unless the quotes are part of the literal.
- For literal strings, keep sentence punctuation outside the quoted literal when including it inside would change the value.

## Periods and exclamation marks

- End complete prose sentences with appropriate punctuation.
- Do not put periods in headings.
- Use one space after a period.
- Avoid exclamation marks in technical, reference, and procedural content.

## Slashes

- Prefer `and` or `or` to `/` in prose.
- Do not use slash abbreviations such as `w/`.
- Use slashes where they are part of technical syntax, URLs, or paths.
- Avoid `and/or` unless severe space constraints make it necessary.

## Numbers

- In general prose, spell out zero through nine and use numerals for 10 and above.
- Use numerals for technical quantities, versions, measurements, percentages, prices, dimensions, negative numbers, decimals, mathematical expressions, and similar precise values.
- If small and large numbers appear together in the same comparison, numerals for all can improve consistency.
- Spell out ordinal numbers in prose unless the context requires a numeral.
- Avoid starting a sentence with a numeral; rewrite when practical.
- Prefer decimal fractions when they are clearer for the domain.
- Use numerals plus `%` for percentages except when a spelled-out form is required at the start of a sentence.
- Use Arabic rather than Roman numerals unless the source or proper name requires Roman numerals.

## Dates and times

- Prefer unambiguous dates such as `January 19, 2017` in prose.
- Use ISO `YYYY-MM-DD` for numeric-only dates.
- Use a four-digit year when the year matters.
- Put the date before the time when both appear.
- Use `AM` and `PM` in 12-hour prose time with a space before them; omit `:00` for exact hours when appropriate.
- Use 24-hour time when the product, UI, data, or code already uses it.
- Avoid seasons for globally relevant timing; use months or quarters.
- Avoid time-zone abbreviations when they could be ambiguous; identify the region or UTC offset when necessary.

## Units

- Put a space between a number and most unit symbols.
- Do not add plural `s` to unit symbols.
- Distinguish decimal and binary byte units when technically relevant (`GB` versus `GiB`).
- Prefer `per` to `/` in prose rates when space permits.
- Repeat the unit on both sides of a range when omission could be ambiguous.
- Make currencies unambiguous when multiple countries use the same symbol.

## Lists and punctuation

- Capitalize the first word of normal numbered and bulleted items.
- End full-sentence items with periods.
- Omit periods for consistently short fragments, single words, pure code, or link-only items.
- If one item requires sentence punctuation, consider punctuating all items for consistency.

## Examples

- Use examples to clarify a concept, not to decorate prose.
- Prefer realistic, safe, non-PII examples.
- Introduce long examples in their own sentence or paragraph.
- Avoid generic `foo`/`bar` names when meaningful names make the example easier to understand.
