# Core writing rules

Use this reference for substantial drafting or editing where the compact rules in `SKILL.md` are not enough.

## Reader and purpose

- Write for the reader's task and likely knowledge level.
- Give each document, section, and paragraph a clear purpose.
- Provide context before detail, but do not delay the answer with generic background.
- Put the most important information first in sentences and paragraphs.
- Prefer prescriptive guidance when a reader needs a path forward. Recommend the most relevant path rather than presenting an undifferentiated option dump.
- When multiple valid paths matter, distinguish them by concrete trade-offs.

## Voice and tone

- Be conversational, respectful, direct, and professional.
- Let clarity carry the tone; do not manufacture friendliness with filler.
- Use "you" for the reader and imperative verbs for instructions.
- Avoid organizational "we" unless the organization itself is the clear actor.
- Prefer active voice. Use passive voice only when the actor is irrelevant, unknown, or less important than the object.
- Use present tense for stable behavior. Use future tense only for genuinely later events.
- Common two-word contractions are fine; avoid nonstandard or three-word contractions.
- Avoid unnecessary "please" in procedural instructions.

## Sentence construction

- Prefer subject-verb-object order.
- Keep the subject and verb close together.
- Put a condition, goal, or context before the action it qualifies.
- Avoid long strings of noun modifiers; rewrite when more than two modifiers make parsing difficult.
- Include helper words such as "that", "which", "of", and articles when they improve comprehension.
- Clarify pronoun antecedents. Prefer "this setting" over a bare "this" when ambiguity is possible.
- Use "that" for restrictive clauses and "which" for nonrestrictive clauses when the distinction matters.
- End sentences with prepositions when that is the natural, clear construction.
- Avoid double negatives and nested exceptions.

## Paragraphs

- Keep each paragraph focused on one idea.
- Prefer several short paragraphs over a wall of text.
- A one-sentence paragraph is acceptable when the idea deserves emphasis or isolation.
- A paragraph longer than roughly five or six sentences is a signal to check whether it contains multiple ideas.
- Do not force line breaks inside normal prose.

## Headings and navigation

- Use sentence case.
- Make headings specific enough to predict the content below them.
- Use bare infinitives for tasks and noun phrases for concepts.
- Avoid starting headings with gerunds when a stronger task or concept label exists.
- Avoid numbering headings just to imply sequence; use document structure or a numbered procedure.
- Avoid links inside headings.
- Define an abbreviation in the first paragraph after a heading if the heading must contain an unfamiliar abbreviation.

## Lists

- Use numbered lists for ordered steps or priorities.
- Use bullets for unordered items.
- Use description lists for term-description pairs when the output format supports them.
- Introduce a list with a complete contextual sentence.
- Keep items parallel in grammar, capitalization, punctuation, and conceptual scope.
- Do not add "etc." merely to signal that a list is incomplete; make the introduction say that the list contains examples or common cases.

## Tables

- Use a table when readers need to compare multiple attributes across items.
- Prefer a list for one-dimensional information.
- Keep column headings concise and in sentence case.
- Avoid merged cells and layout-only tables in HTML.
- Sort rows logically or alphabetically when no stronger order exists.
- Split a table if it becomes too wide, dense, or difficult to scan.
- Do not put essential information only in icons, color, or images inside a table.

## Links and references

- Prefer giving the necessary information in place when it is short and important.
- Use descriptive link text that states the destination topic.
- Avoid "click here", bare URLs, and repeated redundant links.
- Explain unusual link behavior such as a download or opening a different application.
- Put the context before the link: "For deployment limits, see ..." rather than "See ... for deployment limits."

## Editing for economy

Remove or rewrite:

- pre-announcements such as "In this section, we'll discuss" when the heading already says it;
- filler such as "it is important to note that";
- duplicate conclusions;
- unnecessary qualifiers such as "very", "really", "basically", and "just";
- vague verbs such as "utilize" when "use" is enough;
- weak capability phrasing such as "allows you to" when a direct verb works;
- needless nominalizations when a verb is clearer;
- repeated context the reader already has.

Do not shorten text at the cost of ambiguity, missing articles, unclear pronouns, or hidden conditions.

## Evidence and judgment

- Separate observations from interpretations and recommendations.
- Do not use style to disguise uncertainty.
- Place source attribution or evidence next to consequential claims when the context requires it.
- State assumptions that materially affect the result.
- Avoid excessive claims, especially around security, cost, reliability, performance, uniqueness, and superiority.
- Prefer measurable language over superlatives.

## Timelessness

Avoid time-sensitive words when they add no necessary information:

- currently
- latest
- new
- now
- soon
- eventually
- future
- as of this writing

When time matters, anchor it to a date, version, release, or explicit event.
